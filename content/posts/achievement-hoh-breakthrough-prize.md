Fundamental physics moves slowly, and it moves in teams. A result that finally arrives after a decade of construction, calibration and analysis belongs to the thousands of people who built the apparatus and checked each other's work — which is why the Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics is awarded to collaborations rather than to individuals.

[Assoc. Prof. Dr. Hoh Siew Yan](/blog/staff-hoh-siew-yan) of the Department of Physics appears on two of those laureate lists, in consecutive years: with the **CMS Collaboration at CERN** for the 2025 prize, and with the **Muon g-2 collaboration** for the 2026 prize. The two recognitions come from different experiments, on different continents, at different stages of his career.

## 2025 — measuring the Higgs boson

The 2025 prize recognised the CMS Collaboration, one of the two general-purpose experiments built around the Large Hadron Collider, for its measurements of Higgs boson properties, its searches for rare processes and matter–antimatter asymmetry, and its exploration of matter at the most extreme conditions ever produced in a laboratory.

The Higgs boson is the quantum excitation of the Higgs field — the mechanism by which fundamental particles acquire mass. Measuring its mass, its spin and its couplings to other particles is how we test whether the Standard Model actually holds together, and where it might not.

Dr. Hoh joined CMS during his PhD, carried out jointly at the **University of Padova** and **Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia**, which placed him inside one of the largest scientific collaborations in the world at an early stage of his research career.

## 2026 — the muon's magnetic moment

The 2026 prize went to the co-authors of the Muon g-2 publications from CERN, Brookhaven and Fermilab.

A muon behaves like a tiny bar magnet, precessing in a magnetic field. Quantum theory predicts that the rate of that precession should differ from the naive value by a small, calculable amount — and *every* particle in nature contributes to that difference, including particles nobody has discovered yet. Measure the deviation precisely enough and you are, in effect, taking a photograph of all of physics at once.

Successive experiments at CERN, then Brookhaven, then Fermilab refined that measurement across six decades. The latest results reach a precision of 140 parts per billion, using a 50-foot superconducting storage ring originally built at CERN and shipped by road and sea to Illinois. The gap between measurement and theory has not closed, and what it means is still one of the genuinely open questions in the field.

Dr. Hoh contributed to this work during his postdoctoral research at the **Tsung-Dao Lee Institute, Shanghai Jiao Tong University**.

## Physics at the scale of petabytes

What both experiments share, and what is easy to miss in any account of the physics, is the sheer quantity of data involved.

The LHC produces over 600 million proton–proton collisions every second, generating raw data at roughly a petabyte per second. A real-time trigger system throws away almost all of it, keeping only what looks scientifically interesting — and even after that filtering, CMS alone produces more than five petabytes a year, distributed across a computing grid spanning more than 100 institutions in 36 countries. Muon g-2 is a far smaller experiment, and still records data at 18 gigabytes per second, processed live by 24 GPUs in parallel.

Working at that scale demands more than physics. It demands distributed computing, statistical inference, the extraction of faint signals from overwhelming noise, and collaborative software development across time zones — skills that experimental particle physicists acquire largely out of necessity.

## Bringing the method into the classroom

Dr. Hoh teaches **PHY408: Data Intensive Science** at Xiamen University Malaysia, which uses frontier experiments — the LHC, gravitational wave detectors, neutrino oscillation experiments, muon g-2 — as live case studies in how physicists manage and extract meaning from enormous datasets. The course is built on a *physics first, then code* philosophy: the working norms of large collaborations, including version control, blind analysis, cross-checks and careful treatment of systematic uncertainty, are taught as practice rather than as principle.

A second strand runs in a less obvious direction. His course **G0385: From Physics to Finance**, and his research in econophysics, apply the same quantitative toolkit to financial markets. The connection is closer than it looks: markets, like particle collisions, are high-dimensional, noisy, and badly described by simple distributions. Monte Carlo simulation, stochastic modelling, maximum likelihood estimation and random matrix theory transfer directly. A physicist trained on real detector data is also instinctively suspicious of the assumptions — normality, independence — that conventional financial models tend to take for granted.

## Two directions with regional relevance

**Muography** uses naturally occurring cosmic-ray muons to image the inside of large geological structures, much as X-rays image a body but on the scale of a landscape. Developed for volcano monitoring and for looking inside pyramids, it has real potential for mineral exploration, infrastructure monitoring and hazard assessment in a geologically complex region.

**Laser wakefield acceleration** aims to shrink particle accelerators from kilometres to a tabletop. Ultrashort, intense laser pulses fired into a plasma set up accelerating fields thousands of times stronger than conventional technology allows, which opens a path to compact radiation sources for medical imaging, cancer therapy and accessible experimental physics.

These prizes belong first to the many hundreds of scientists who built and ran two of the most complex experiments ever attempted. Dr. Hoh's inclusion in both — through work rooted in Padova, Kuala Lumpur and Shanghai — reflects a career spent at that frontier, and brings to the department a set of methods and a culture of rigorous data science alongside the recognition.
